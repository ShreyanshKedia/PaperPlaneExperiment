PAPER PLANE
EXPERIMENT

How does the design of a paper plane affect its flight distance, stability, and speed?

EXPLORE WATCH VIDEOS

Aim and variables

This experiment has a lot of possible variables: wind, the height of the launcher, the force of the launcher, the angle of the launcher, the type of aircraft launched, the wingspan, the wing area and even the mass of the plane. Only a select few were chosen.

The aim is to understand how the wing area (in cm²) of a paper plane and its type (glider or dart) relate to the distance it flies (in metres) in a controlled environment, with the effect of every other variable removed.

Method

All aircraft were launched by an aircraft launcher set at an angle of 15° and using the same elastic force. The launcher was kept 50 cm above the ground.

The paper planes were launched indoors to remove the effect of changing wind speed and direction, another variable associated with this experiment.

What the results show

This experiment shows that, with these prerequisites and conditions, gliders tend to be more effective in terms of pure distance. “Mighty Eagle”, a glider that prioritises pure wing area, performed the best, reaching around 6.85 metres on average.

This is in sharp contrast to the worst performer, “Arrowhead”. Being a front-heavy, dart-style paper aircraft, it needs a very high initial speed for its wings to generate enough lift to support the 5 grams the plane weighs, especially as its wing area is only 114.1 cm².

The Classic Dart, while not achieving the greatest performance, did quite well for a dart-type aircraft, owing to its large wing area and less weight at the nose. This highlights the difference again: if we hold the aircraft type constant and compare the Classic Dart with the Arrowhead, we can show that nose weight and wing area are two factors that decide the aerodynamic efficiency of a dart-style paper plane.

Designing the launcher

Interestingly, the hardest part of the experiment for me was designing the paper plane launcher. I failed twice before perfecting the design, learning something new each time. The final iteration (V3) featured many improvements over the first two.

What changed in Version 3

A full locking feature. I could let go of the rubber band and the energy would remain stored. This allowed me to load the aircraft with one arm and secure the launcher with the other, increasing accuracy and consistency.

A set guide rail, independent of the trigger. Versions 1 and 2 used an “M” shaped piece of paper and cardboard respectively. The elastic was secured at the lower symmetrical point of the M, and was chambered by compressing the shape and stretching the elastic to the other end of the M. Version 3 has an independent chambering and firing mechanism: a lock made of a toothpick, fastened between the two parallel pieces of cardboard, holds the pusher in place while the paper plane is placed freely. To launch, the user unpins the toothpick, which automatically launches the plane without changing the distance between the two guide rails, unlike previous iterations.

A cardboard pusher. In earlier iterations the thin elastic band violently ripped pieces of paper from the plane, increasing drag and reducing the distance flown. In Version 3 the pusher spreads the force from the thin band over a long, large area, since pressure is inversely proportional to area. This had two desired consequences: it stopped the ripping, and it increased accuracy because all parts of the plane accelerated equally and the airframe couldn’t bend.

What I learned

This struggle showed me what aeronautical engineering is actually about. It’s not about the theory, it’s the application, perseverance and ingenuity. As soon as I tried something original, all my problems with the original copied design disappeared.
